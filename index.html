Lesson 3 · Business presentation

How to build a talk your classmates can follow

You have a topic, and you have started reading about it. This lesson shows you what to do next, one small skill at a time.

The lesson has five parts. Each part takes 15 to 30 minutes. The whole lesson takes about two hours, so do it in a few sittings. Your answers save on this device.

In every skill you will write one line for your own talk. At the end, your lines become a one page talk plan with a completion code.

The photos in this lesson were made with AI image tools. They show no real people. The slides come from my sample talk, The Cloud Next Door.

Meet Emily and Kevin. You will follow them through the whole lesson.

EmilyA first semester student. She is the speaker.
KevinHer classmate. He listens from row three.

Emily is in her first semester. She has never given a talk before. For two weeks she has been reading about her topic: how a social media app decides which posts you see.

Kevin is her classmate. He sits in row three. He came to class straight from work. He uses Instagram every day. He has read nothing about how it works.

Emily and Kevin are made up. Their problems are real. Emily’s first try has the same problems that most first talks have.

Emily’s topic is not on our topic list. So everyone can practice on it.

I love Scandinavian Christmas design in homes.

Here is how to turn a topic into one question, in five small steps.

EmilyA first semester student. She is the speaker.
1

Write your topic.

Use just a few words.

Emily writes: Social media algorithms.

2

Ask yourself: what changed?

A good talk is about one change.

Emily writes: In January 2018, Facebook changed how it picks the posts you see.

3

Ask yourself: who feels that change?

Name the people.

Emily writes: Everyone who scrolls.

4

Put the change and the people into one question.

You can borrow this shape: When ___ happens, what happens to ___?

Emily writes: When an app is built to keep you scrolling, what does it show you more of?

5

Test your question three ways.

Can you say it in one breath? Is the answer more than yes or no? Could you answer it in about four typed pages?

Emily writes: Yes, yes, and yes.

Now Emily has a way to choose. For every fact, she asks: does this help answer my question? The history of Facebook? The laws? The future of AI? They stay in her notes.

Part 1 of 5 · Your story

Skill 1 of 11: ask one question. 💡 Learn why it works.

A question can be too big, too small, or about right for ten minutes.

🌍

Too big

“How has social media changed the world?” You would need a whole semester. In ten minutes you could only list facts.

🤏

Too small

“Does Facebook choose which posts you see?” The answer is “yes.” Your talk is over in ten seconds.

✅

About right

“When an app is built to keep you scrolling, what does it show you more of?” It is about one change. It names the people. It takes a few minutes to answer well.

Here is a quick check. Imagine your answer as four typed pages. If you would need forty pages, the question is too big. If you would need four lines, it is too small.

Part 2 of 5 · Your words

Skill 4 of 11: start from zero and explain every big word. 👀 Look at a first try.

Emily has read ten articles. Kevin has read none. Her second minute forgets that.

EmilyA first semester student. She is the speaker.
1

“As everyone knows.” Emily knows, because she read about it for two weeks. Kevin does not know. Most of the class does not know.

2

“Meta.” Kevin knows Instagram. He knows Facebook. He may not know that one company owns both, and that the company is called Meta. Emily never tells him.

3

Three big words in a row. Algorithm means the software that picks which posts you see. Optimizes means it tries to get as much as it can of one thing. Engagement means every time you tap, comment, share, or stop to look.

A big word is any word your classmates might not know. It can be long, like “algorithm.” It can also sound easy, like “engagement.” Kevin knows that word from weddings. He does not know what it means here.

This happens to everyone. After you learn something, it is hard to remember what it was like not to know it.

Here is how to give both sides a fair turn.

EmilyA first semester student. She is the speaker.
1

Name your two sides.

Almost every topic on our list has these two: the company, and the people affected.

2

Find the company’s own words.

Look for what the company said to a reporter, or a statement on its own website.

Emily writes: Mark Zuckerberg says advertisers do not want their ads next to angry posts.

3

Check that it is fair.

Ask: would the company agree that this is what it said? If yes, it is fair.

4

Give each side its own sentence.

Start one with “The company says.” Start the other with “The people affected say.”

Emily writes: A former worker says the company knew how to make the apps safer and chose not to.

5

Pick the side you will spend more time on.

Choose the side of the story you can bring to life best. Then give the other side its turn.

One rule about numbers: always pair data from the company’s side of the story with reporting from an outlet with editors, a credible source.
